About the sundew identifier

This is an identification aid, not an authority. It suggests which of 110 sundew (Drosera) species a photo shows. It only knows these 110 species. It cannot tell when a photo shows something else, like another plant or a sundew not on its list, and it may still suggest a sundew name, sometimes with high confidence. Check any identification with an expert before using it for legal, trade or conservation decisions.

On this page: How it works · Reading a result · Taking a good photo · Privacy · The model · Species pages · Licences and credits

How it works

  1. The first time you open the identifier, your browser downloads the model (about 22 MB) and the code that runs it, and keeps them for next time.
  2. When you choose a photo, your browser turns it upright, shrinks it to 255 pixels on the short side and takes the 224 × 224 centre, exactly as the photos were prepared when the model was trained.
  3. The model scores the photo against each of the 110 species it knows. The scores become percentages that add up to 100%.
  4. A fixed rule decides what to say: a species, a section, or "not sure". It is described below, and it is the same rule for every photo.

All of this happens on your device. Nothing about the photo, or the result, is sent anywhere.

How to read a result

  • Best match among 110 sundew species: the model gave one species at least 65% of the probability, and the photo matched its 110 species strongly overall. In testing on photos of the listed species, about 9 in 10 of these answers were right.
  • Not sure of the species. Probably section X: no single species was likely enough, but the species of one section (a group of related sundews) together got at least 70%.
  • Not sure: neither of the above. The possible matches still include the right species most of the time, but the first one is right only about half the time.

The five most likely species are always shown with their percentages. Percentages near the 65% line can differ slightly between devices, so the same photo may get an answer on one phone and "Not sure" on another. "Not sure" can also come with a high first percentage: that happens when the photo doesn't look much like any of the 110 species overall, which is typical of photos of other plants.

Species with few training photos are identified less reliably: in testing, about 8 in 10 of their confident answers were right. The full numbers are in the evaluation report. They come from photos of the 110 listed species. In a separate test with photos of other plants, the tool still named a sundew for about 1 in 10 of them, and for about 1 in 3 photos of sundew species outside its list (open-set test).

Taking a good photo

  • One plant, filling most of the frame. The identifier looks only at the centre square of the photo, so keep the plant there.
  • A clear photo with the leaves in focus, taken from above or the side.
  • Its labelled training photos are iNaturalist observations of wild plants (cultivated plants were left out), so photos of potted plants, for example under grow lights, may be less like what it has seen.
  • A JPEG. Some browsers cannot open HEIC photos from iPhones; share or export them as JPEG first.

Your photo and your privacy

Your photo is processed in your browser and is not uploaded. The model and the code that runs it are downloaded once from this site, and the identification happens on your device. The site does not read the location stored in your photo (it uses only the orientation tag, to turn the photo upright), and has no analytics, cookies or tracking. Everything the page loads (the model, the code, the fonts and the photos) comes from this site; links to other sites, such as iNaturalist, are only followed if you click them. An example photo is downloaded from this site when you press it, like any other image on the site. As with any website, the host (GitHub Pages) receives your IP address when you load the page.

The model

sundew-species v1.0.0 by Pierce Taylor: a DINOv2-S image classifier, distilled from a larger DINOv2-L model and trained on 23,244 iNaturalist photos of 110 Drosera species. See the model card for how it was trained and tested.

On validation photos it had never trained on, the site's rule gave a confident answer for 71.6% of photos, and 91.5% of those answers were right (evaluation report).

Species pages

Each of the 110 species has a page with photos, its section, where it was first described, and how well the identifier recognises it on validation photos, including the species it is most often mistaken for. The original publication of each name comes from the International Plant Names Index (IPNI). For a species that was first described under another name, the page cites both that first description and the publication that gave it its current name. When the Biodiversity Heritage Library has scanned the page, there is a link to read it.
